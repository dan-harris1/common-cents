    The Question of Money

    The question at hand, ‘what is money,’ is not to ask what is the optimum form of money, but weather it has any form at all. Is money something we hold in our hand, or in our head? Is it something we need to believe in and trust, or something we learn and then know. We’ve been taught that money is something that is decided collectively, but what if it were something we do individually, like math? 

    Before there was money (p22), we bartered, or so we are told. This is actually a myth concocted to conceal the essence of money. Money’s genesis is one of many myths promulgated with the ulterior purpose of justifying the existence of a ruler. This shouldn’t be surprising. ‘His-story’ is written by a victor, not an unbiased observer. Any account of the past that doesn’t take this into account, is remiss.

    Those who rule have worked diligently to expunge all evidence of any society having ever existed without an omnipotent ruler. They also fund academia to ensure that the lack of such evidence be interpreted as proof of humanity’s need to be ruled. Conquerers have, as a matter of course, supplanted the historical records of the vanquished, to justify their rule of the present. This is the context whence the prevailing definition of money comes.

    We’re taught that money is a form of payment. This seems too obvious to refute, but it’s obvious in the same way the sun goes around the earth. If we set aside what empiricism suggests, and consider what’s logically imperative, our perception of money changes just as profoundly as our perception of the solar system.

    Clearly, money is useful, but exactly how? It appears to solve a problem, but what problem does it actually solve? Economists say money solves the problem posed by the improbability of two people having reciprocal desires. They reason that money’s value arises from it being a commonly coveted form of payment. So, why isn’t this money’s definition? Dictionaries identify money as a form of payment, then needlessly complicate this notion by describing it as a medium of exchange (p20). Why? This bizarre expression is neither a useful abstraction nor an enlightened explanation of a payment. Why don’t dictionaries define money as simply a ‘commonly accepted form of payment’?

    This peculiarity is where the question of money rightly begins. It’s merely a first question because any diligent inquiry into money’s essence will quickly reveal that it does not exist in isolation. The very notion of money, is indelibly linked to the definitions of morality and law.

    Consider the nature of the question itself. Is money’s definition necessarily decided, or can it be deduced? If the former, then money is whatever someone, like a ruler, decrees it to be; its definition is completely arbitrary! If the latter, then money’s definition is not arbitrary, and must be discovered by a process of reasoning.

    It’s crucial to appreciate how stultifying the first proposition is to recognizing the existence of the second. There’s no impetus to doubt the definition of money, if money is a form of payment — by definition! If, however, we regard this orthodox definition as a mere hypothesis, instead of a definition, we allow for there to be a heterodox hypothesis... one predicated on reason rather than any kind of ruling.

    What would it mean if the definition of money was, not just far from arbitrary, but a logical imperative? This second question is so much more profound than the first, as to almost answer it. When money’s definition is decreed, instead of deduced, our conception of it can be manipulated.

    There are two ways to respond to the question ‘what is money?’ The first is to tout the virtues of cowrie shells, tally sticks, gold coins, legal tender, and/or digital tokens. This response assumes money is a form of payment. The second way is to assume nothing and endeavor to identify the problem it solves. This is the primordial question of money.

    What problem does money solve? The orthodox diagnosis is the improbability of two people having a coincidences of wants (p4). The heterodox diagnosis, being introduced here, is the measurement problem (p7). When trying to figure out which is more meritorious, it isn’t enough to evaluate just the impediment, we need also to evaluate the milieu each remedy give rise to.

    Legal or Lawful?

    The term ‘legal’ is often used synonymously with ‘lawful,’ yet the two terms don’t mean the same thing at all. Indeed, they should never be used interchangeably. When conflate them, we lose the ability to distinguish between permission from a principal, and a principle, which is a paradigm in Nature.

    A legal definition is decreed. A lawful definition is discovered. It’s easy to keep this distinction clear by remembering that a rule comes from a ruler, and a law comes from God, Gaia, or Nature, depending on your worldview. Rulers rely on our inability to discern any difference. To this end, principals call their edicts statutes, and define a statute as a law. This has two effects. It obfuscates the synonymousness of law with principle, and it equates the legitimacy of a rule with a law.

    While the ill effect of semantic imprecision is incalculable, what’s most apropos to this discourse is how ambiguity is used to misconstrue our conception of law, then morality, and ultimately money. Rectifying the rhetoric of rulers requires our rationality be rooted in natural philosophy.

    Broadly speaking, natural philosophy aims to explain the natural behavior of things. Here, things is the operative word. Every thing has its own nature with which it comports naturally... effortlessly. The earliest eastern philosophers referred to this phenomena as ‘the way,’ i.e., Daoism. The earliest philosophers in the west figured out that it was possible to describe the way of some natural phenomena with fanatically precise semantics. The first thing they figured out was the way of reason itself. Aristotle, Pythagoras, and Euclid, deduced and articulated the rules of logic.

    A thousand years later, Galileo, Decartes, and Newton, devised the scientific method. This allowed them to figure out the nature of inanimate things. Physics, chemistry, and astronomy, are referred to as hard sciences for how adamantly they rely on this methodology. Biology, zoology, and physiology, are referred to as softer sciences because the behavior of living organisms cannot be predicted as precisely by the scientific method. Living things act autonomously, adapt, and evolve. When we try to explain human nature, we encounter free-will. Explaining our nature is vastly more complicated because our will is informed by our self-awareness.

    By the time epistemology contends with sociology, the rigor of the scientific method has softened enough to permit pseudoscience. The salient difference between pseudoscience and science isn’t fidelity to the scientific method, but to our will to our confirm personal bias. Insofar as it cannot be eliminated, confirmation bias is normal, but it must be acknowledged and accommodated. Intellectual dishonesty arises, and pseudoscience ensues, when it isn’t.

    Whereas pseudoscience is used to confirm a bias, not all efforts to confirm a bias give rise to pseudoscience. This is to say, not all biases are bad. A predilection for individual sovereignty, for example, isn’t a bad — unless, you are in the business of ruling, or suffering Stockholm Syndrome.

    Authoritarianism

    Authoritarianism is the advocacy and enforcement of obedience to a ruler at the expense of individual sovereignty. While this proposition may be essential during wartime, in peacetime authoritarianism is a pernicious philosophy. Whether it is called socialism, communism, or totalitarianism, any form of collectivism is an interpolation of authoritarianism.

    Ruling is the business of taxing. Taxing is stealing by another name. Immoral means nothing if not involuntary trade, i.e. theft. We need only recognize how concise the moral case against ruling actually is, to realize how intellectually dishonest the authoritarianism philosophy necessarily is. It is the antithesis of the way of natural philosophy!

    The occupation of authoritarians is to couch every social woe in a manner that promotes the need for an omnipotent ruler. Since authoritarians cannot openly acknowledge their intent, pundits rely on pseudoscience to persuade. Misdiagnosing the problem money solves, allows authoritarians to advocate for monopolizing what we tender as payment, without ever having to explicitly say so.

    coincidences of wants

    Economists surmise the primordial impediment to trade is the improbability of two people having disposable possessions that coincide with what the other desires to possess.

    By what metric or means can we assess how meritorious this diagnosis is? Not every proposition that’s true, can be proven. So, let’s compare the merit of the diagnosis, with the ramification of the remedy it suggests.

    merit of the diagnosis

    The improbability of a coincidence of wants seems a reasonable diagnosis, today, because of the myriad of products and services that technology now makes available. This diagnosis becomes increasingly less meritorious, however, the further back in history we look. Prior to the industrial revolution, people lived in with unrelenting scarcity. There were few things to trade. Consequently, a wider variety of things were tendered as payment.

    Since the industrial revolution, innovation has accelerated, causing the scarcity of the past increasingly more difficult your imagination. Consequently, we aren’t more skeptical in the present, about the problem money is said to have solved in the past.

    We’re also told money was conceived by the will of a free market. Yet, history records no society having ever been free from the will of a ruler. It isn’t just remiss, it is ridiculous to assume that whatever notion of money arose originally, naturally, wasn’t usurped to facilitate taxation. A shekel was a bushel of barley before it was a metal coin, and a dollar was a coin of gold before it was a paper bill. It was not a free market that compelled such transmutations.

    ramification of the diagnosis

    The improbability of any two people having a coincidence of wants, suggests there be a common form or payment. The ramification of this diagnosis is for there to be fewer and fewer forms of payment, until there is single elixir of commerce.

    The orthodox diagnosis [of the problem money solves], provides rulers the subterfuge needed to justify the monopolization of what is tendered as payment. A ruler’s monopoly of violence is thus buttressed by a monetary monopoly, e.g. a central bank.

    The orthodox diagnosis [of the problem money solves] is exemplary of pseudoscience. It necessitates a reliance on a central planner, regulator, or ruler. When then there is only a single form of payment, it’s then possible to institute taxes that provide the ruler granular control over individuals.

    The scientific method entails rival explanations and reproducible experiments to determine which worldview better comports to Natural Law. In the absence of a competing concepts, there is no datum to delineate legal dogma from lawful definition.

    Anarchism

    Anarchism is the advocacy and enforcement of individual sovereignty at the expense of authoritarianism. Etymologically, ‘anarchy’ means ‘without a ruler’ not chaos. We’ve been inculcated by rulers to believe that the absence of rulers [not rules!] will lead to chaos. In reality, the opposite is true. Chaos is unmitigated aggression. Aggression is the initiation of force. The absence of aggression is peace. Where authoritarianism is the monopolization of aggression, anarchism is its abolition.

    We need only acknowledge how coherent the philosophy undergirding anarchy is, to realize the kind of gaslighting, brainwashing, or Jedi mind trick, that’s required to hide it. Whereas the American revolution was inspired by an anarchist manifesto, the United States is governed by an authoritarian constitution. Which philosophy was the nation actually founded on? It is more than a little ironic that a nation founded on the postulate ‘all men are created equal and are endowed by God (or Gaia, or Nature) with certain un-alien-able rights,’ must have the power to place a lien on them!

    The notion of a human right is a 17th century attempt at articulating principles that obsolete our reliance on principals, i.e. the ‘rule of law.’ The difference between the two postulates is exemplified by the difference between how principals define money, and a principled definition of money.

    The primordial impediment to trade is the challenge of each party to figure out how much of what they have to sell, should they trade for whatever they wish to buy. The crux of the problem is the seemingly impossible task of objectively measuring what is quintessentially subjective, our opinion of worth. If we were not already doing this routinely everyday, there would be no reason to believe such was even possible.

    Conspicuously missing from the orthodox explanation of money, is any explanation of how exactly we are able to determine anything’s worth. It is no small irony that economists have long sought, and have utterly failed, to find a theory of value. While economists have been looking for a new means for determining any thing’s price, they should’ve been looking for an explanation for how we have done so since time immemorial. How are each of us able to figure out what anything is worth?

    measurement problem

    The measurement problem is the challenge of individual’s to assess the relative worth of what they buy and sell.

    To appreciate just how primordial this impediment to trade actual is, try describing the price of something, anything, without referring to dollars, euros, pesos, or any other legal form of payment. Take notice of your cognitive effort. The strain on your brain, is the merit of the heterodoxical diagnosis of the problem solved by money.

    merit of the diagnosis

    The measurement problem predates bartering. Bartering isn’t trade without money. Money is the cognition by which we barter. It’s the arithmetic process by which we equilibrate our personal preferences. Absent the ability to juxtapose our own desires, we couldn’t reconcile our own preferences, with preferences of others, and strike a bargain. Voluntary trade would be impossible.

    A trade is only made voluntarily when both parties believe they will be better off after doing so. The reasoning with which we juxtapose the worth of what we want, with the worth of what we have, is money. The actual price we pay for anything we buy, is the value we place on whatever we sell in the bargain. When we negotiate the ratio in which we will forgo a quantity of something what we have, for an amount of something we what, we’re actually reconciling our perception of reality, with the perception of a counterparty. A bargain is an instance of intersubjective agreement (p47). Our personal perception of relative worth is recalibrated every time we trade. Rather than explicating this crucial concept, economists obfuscate it by convoluting the notion of opportunity cost.

    The measurement problem is pertinent to every individual and not to any collective. The remedy, money, manifests as a form of logic, not as form of payment. Money is a principle we deduce, not a decree from a principal. Think of the truest, most fundamental meaning of the expression ‘rule of law.’

    ramification of the diagnosis

    Properly understood, money allows us to trade anything for anything. This obviates not only the measurement problem, but the problem posed by the improbability of coincidence-of-wants, and any need for monetary tokens of credit (p13).

    Money, isn’t mysterious or even esoteric... but, alas, it’s known by a different name in different disciplines. The very fact that the algorithm that is money, is foundational to mathematics and physics, is a strong indication that the heterodox definition of money better comports to Natural Law, than does the orthodox definition.

    Religiosity or Rationality?

    It is widely believed that money is a system of belief. We intuit as much as children. Before we’re five, we’ve figured out that coins, though made of metal, aren’t as good as bills made of paper, and that some bills are far better than others, though they may be no bigger or prettier. Still more astonishing is that our personal preference for a particular coin or bill has no bearing on how good it is. How strange! On what basis, then, are they good at all? The obvious answer, the answer we surmise in adolescence, is that something’s worth is determined by the desire of others, not our own. Empirically, it appears that money is a token of envy... but envy is one of the seven biblical sins!

    There are many logical inconsistencies that alert us to something being precisely askew about money. Consider the social conditioning required to convince hundreds of millions of people that a single bill imprinted with $20, is exactly twenty times more valuable than a no prettier bill imprinted with $1. This isn’t alarming to us as children because we’re adept at pretending. Clearly, trade is a game akin to poker, that’s played with tokens called money, but not played for fun nor even voluntarily.

    The extent to which a monetary token is prerequisite to all trade, is the extent to which it represents, not value per se, but permission to participate in a economy. This is the unspoken essence of a system-of-credit (p9). What’s critical is not that we believe worthless tokens are valuable, but that we believe their use is necessary for commerce.

    Herein, the terms religiosity and rationality are used to reference two epistemological dispositions. Religiosity refers to the disposition that relies on principals, not to any sense of spirituality, the realm hereafter, nor the existence of God. Rationality refers to the disposition that relies on principles, not to intelligence, expertise, nor education. Its crucial to be cognizant of this distinction whenever discerning moral and immoral behavior. To illustrate, let’s explicate the meaning of coercion, and the meaning of a moral authority.

    Coercion has necessarily two forms, fraud and force, and the initiation of either form can be either moral or immoral. Whereas aggression is the immoral initiation of coercion, justification is the moral initiation of coercion. The question of morality thus reduces to ‘whence does the definition of morality come?’ We can either look for a principal to decide what is and isn’t moral, or we can look for a principle that explains the nature of humans. Notice how this framing perfectly aligns with the question of money!

    System-of-credit

    Notwithstanding how familiar we all are with it, credit is an exceedingly abstract concept. As with money, we intuit our conception of it as children. Credit is something given in lieu of payment. Regal trophies, blue ribbons, smiley stickers, and letter grades, are all used to confer accreditation, not payment. While this distinction may seem merely academic, it’s actually both a semantical and logical imperative.

    If I promise to pay you later, or I grant you permission to pay me later, or we otherwise agree to defer payment, an instance of credit is invoked. Credit is the obligation that is instantiated whenever settlement is deferred. It exists for the duration of the deferral, from the moment a trade is consummated, until it’s settled. Think of the time between ordering a hotdog from a street vendor and handing the vendor cash, ordering a meal in a restaurant and paying the bill, or paying a remote seller for something, and it being delivered.

    credit

    Credit is permission (p74) that is conferred as a placeholder for payment. When quantized, individual units of permission are most aptly called ‘credits.’ Within a proprietary system, however, credits are named, or branded. Credits are reified by a metaphysical process called tokenization (p11).

    A system of commerce based on credit, facilitates commerce by collectivizing, or – ‘socializing’ – credit per se. It’s successful when its brand of credit is prerequisite to all trade in a given territory; when it is a monopoly. Canada and the United States have both monopolized the brand “dollar,” for example, yet neither can control the brand “Bitcoin” even in their own territory.

    Systems of credit rely on religiosity to indoctrinate. Today, people are duped into believing we need to rely on a principal to issue and administer a supply of social credits. This is merely the latest incantation of the con-fidence game. Historically, principals won our capitulation by appealing to us spirituality, rather than economically. In either zeitgeist, their con is the same; coerce a population into believing it necessary to ‘voluntarily’ pay a tribute, tithe, or tax, to a ruler, viceroy, or authority. As repugnant to morality as this may be, reality is worse still... but we’ll defer an explanation of usury for now.

    Ostensibly, systems of credit exist to facilitate commerce, not trade. In reality, they exit to control and plunder a population. Whether the tokens representing credit are made of wood, metal, paper, or are wholly imaginary, is largely irrelevant to the system’s overt function, but profoundly relevant to its covert function.

    System-of-merit

    Merit, while not as abstract as credit, does require some effort to understand. Colloquially, it’s routinely referred to as intrinsic value. This is unfortunate since intrinsic value is among the defunct theories of value. Still, there is the obvious sense in which water is good for us to consume and gasoline is good for cars to consume. Merit refers to the virtue or utility that motivates us individually to acquire a product or service.

    merit

    Merit is the tangible benefit that is derived from possessing or consuming, or using a given product or service.

    Whereas the merit of gasoline is the distance it will propel a car, the value an individual places on that merit is wholly dependent on their circumstance and/or preference. If they don’t own a car, or their car isn’t working, they cannot use gasoline’s merit. Does this mean they haven’t any reason to accept gasoline as payment in any given trade? Not at all.

    In a system-of-merit, products and services are traded directly, without any kind of intermediary token-of-credit. Anything can be traded for anything because counterposing offers always equilibrate in some ratio. A trade occurs voluntarily when both parties agree on the ratio, and arrive at a bargain. The proportion in which two things are traded is an objective measure of both parties desire for the merit they perceive.

    The improbability of a coincidence-of-wants is obviated by at least one party tendering a commonly coveted or consumed commodity, or an otherwise more ‘liquid’ form of payment. An individual is much more cognizant of what they accrues as savings, when participating in a system of commerce that is based on merit, than one based on credit. They accrue what’s most tradable, as the wherewithal to acquire what’s least tradable. Liquidity is the fulcrum.

    What’s germane is that a system of trade, based on the merit of what’s traded, relies on the rationality of every individual to decide what is in their own interest. They don’t need to believe a principal to be honorable, nor pretend their worthless tokens are valuable.

    Rationality is based on logic. Logic comports to first-principles. The principle on which the heterodox conception of money is based, is that voluntary trade is moral, and involuntary trade is immoral — theft! While this is simple enough to be understood by all, not all desire morality be so acutely defined… for what then could be said of the difference between stealing and taxing? And... what of the coincidence of wants? When anything can be readily equated for anything, how improbable would a coincidence-of-wants actually be?

    Permission or Prerogative?

    Of all the vying conceptions of money, none is more persistent, nor more pertinent, than that of money being a form of payment. It is ironic that neither the orthodox nor heterodox theory of money require a medium be exchanged. We can see this when we examine how each employs information technology (p12).

    A couple decades before the internet’s public debut, plastic credit and debit cards demonstrated how credit needn’t be represented by metal coins or paper bills. A simple swipe of a magnet strip would do. With the advent of the world wide web, any business could have an online store, and this created a demand for conferring credit without physical tokens, or even the plastic card. Though the obvious solution is to create a digital token, such would be an oxymoron.

    tokenization

    Tokenization is a metaphysical means of reifying a concept, notion, sentiment, or emotion. We tokenize to give material form to ideas, which don’t materially exist, but are nonetheless real. Doing so provides for conceptual things to be conferred.

    At this juncture, we need to delve into metaphysics, again. Not every thing that exists, does so materially. Once more, the operative word is thing. In what sense does the letter ‘e’ or the number three (3) exist? What about emotions like happy and sad, and propositions like permission and prerogative? Such things exist in the same realm as truths that cannot be proven. Rationality requires we learn how to properly contend with things we don’t fully or easily apprehend... lest our consciousness never transcend the confines of materialism.

    information technology

    We fashion a bouquet from flowers to confer admiration, congratulation, or sympathy, which don’t exist materially. Via tokenization, a symbol reifies into an icon, an icon reifies into a token, and a token reifies into a contract. As information technology advances, so too must our understanding of encodement. To tokenize is to change the modality in which information is encoded with technology. A subtle change in encodement can have a substantial impact on fidelity.

    A symbol is becomes an icon when it is ubiquitously understood. Think of the symbology of logos. The golden arches of McDonald’s is disseminated for exactly the same purpose as is the crucifix of Christianity.

    An icon can propagate farther and faster than a mere symbol, but icon’s are subject to being misconstrued. Contemplate the effect of turning the aforementioned icons upside down. Does what each represent, change?

    Now consider the impetus to emboss an icon on a metal ingot. What does this achieve? Exclusivity. Whereas the efficacy of an icon is enhanced by it being easily reproduced, a coin’s efficacy is its ability to preclude unauthorized copying, i.e. counterfeiting. Semantically, a token is a fungible contract. When greater fidelity to what it represents is required, a token can be further tokenized to make it nonfungible. A nonfungible token is a contract.

    Information technology entails more than flipping bits and moving bytes; it must comport with the logically imperative of the rules of representation. Heeding these, the prospect of making a token from software is beyond bizarre. It’s nonsensical. A token is created to imbue a notion or emotion with the physical constraints of materiality. Only when it is localized, i.e. located in a specific place, can it be re-located. Euclidean space isn’t relevant to the realm in which information exists. What we describe as displacement in the physical domain of reality, isn’t a degree of freedom in the domain of data.

    We can see the difference between the laws of these two realms of reality when we compare postal-mail with e-mail. When I send a message via postal-mail, I write it on a piece of paper which is then delivered to the recipient. Sending the message requires me to relinquish my possession of it. I no longer have the message because I no longer possess the token I used to reify it. When I send a message by e-mail, the process is analogous, but the result is quite different. Neither the computer on which I write the message, nor the medium by which the message is transmitted, is delivered to the recipient. Sending the message doesn’t require me to relinquish my possession of it. Though sent, I still have the message. It wasn’t tokenized. I didn’t convey the message; I communicated it.

    Data can be printed, copied, and deleted. That’s all. It can’t be displaced. Software simulates displacement in much the same way movies simulate motion. Registers in a computer’s memory are used like frames in a camera’s filmstrip. Rather than the filmstrip being displaced from one reel to another, data is copied from one register, and written to another. The crucial difference is that writing data to the second register does not require it to be deleted from the first register. To this end, software engineers defer to the computer’s memory management, a.k.a. ‘garbage collection’

    Counterfeiting is to physical tokens,
    as double-spending is to imaginary tokens.

    As highly anticipated as a cashless society has been, it’s odd that monetary ‘tokens’ didn’t enter the monetary lexicon, until Bitcoin made their use obsolete. It is also odd that the brilliance behind Bitcoin would employ the latest technology to implement yet another system for doling out permission, instead of a system for exchanging prerogatives.

    Token-of-credit

    A system-of-credit is instituted to monopolize, not what is tendered as payment, but what is tendered in lieu thereof. This nuance isn’t merely academic. It is a logical imperative. A token cannot itself be what it is purported to represent.

    It follows that, a monetary token cannot represent value and be valuable. This is why economists speak of cash having purchasing power, instead of being valuable. This nuance too, isn’t merely academic. It’s an admission.

    Before we accept the proposition that a monetary token represents commercial value per se, albeit indirectly, we need to appreciate how it is apportioned a magnitude of purchasing power.

    Ask an economist ‘what is it that gives a dollar purchasing power?’ and they’ll likely say ‘the demand for it!’ This non-answer is neither profound nor clever. It is dismissive. Rephrase the question to ‘why is there a demand for dollars?’ and you are far more likely to hear an explanation of the supply-demand-curve, than of a system-of-credit. To misconstrue a token-of-credit for a commonly consumed commodity, is to fall prey to the orthodox subterfuge that is monetarism.

    Rulers manipulate the supply of credits, to control the purchasing power of each credit. A token-of-credit is a permission-slip. Only with it, is one is free participate in the system [of credit], where the system is the economy. Without it, one is relegated to suffer, starve, and perhaps even die, to ensure the system remain a monopoly.

    The magnitude of purchasing power each token represents is directly proportional to how many of them a principal permits to exist at a time. Monetary tokens quantize permission. In the case of the US dollar, such permission can’t be bought; it can only be borrowed. Historically, this practice was a sin called usury... and again, that’s for later.

    8

    Common sense suggests that, before people could read or write, they fashioned artifacts as mnemonic devices. Imagine the difficulty of remembering a list of anything without the ability to write a simple note. This is the milieu, zeitgeist, culture, whence monetary tokens come. The earliest implements were useful like the beads of an abacus, not valuable like jewels in a safe. This is another nontrivial nuance. We’ll need to be more precise with our terms, to reason why.

    token

    Semantically, a token is a tokenized icon. Colloquially, a token is an artifact that confers something immaterial, by imbuing it with physical constraints specifically so it can be displaced and relinquished. In orthodox economic speak, a token is any medium that is exchanged.

    We tokenize to augment our memory. We tokenize an agreement, to reconcile the memory of two parties. This is whence the impetus to create more sophisticated tokens comes.
